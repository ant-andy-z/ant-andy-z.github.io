We use the following notations. 

| Notation                  | Meaning                                                                     |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| $(X, \mathcal{F})$        | A measurable space, where $\mathcal{F}$ is a sigma algebra on $X$.          |
| $\mu$                     | A measure. The measure space it lives in is made explicit by the context.   |
| $f$                       | A mapping. The codomain and domain are made explicit by the context. |
| $\sigma(\mathcal{G})$     | Smallest sigma algebra including $\mathcal{G}$.                             |
| $\sigma(f)$               | Smallest sigma algebra generated by a mapping $f$.                          |
| $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$ | Borel sigma algebra of $\mathbb{R}$.                                                 |
# Measurable Mapping
In analysis, a continuous mapping behaves well on topologies. Let $f: X \to X'$ be continuous. For any open set $O'$ in $X'$, $f^{-1}(O')$ is an open set in $X$. We are now interested in mappings that work well on measurable spaces. 

> **Definition 1: (*measurable*)**
> Let $(X, \mathcal{F}), (X', \mathcal{F'})$ be two measurable spaces. A mapping $f: X \to X'$ is measurable if: 
> $$ f^{-1}(A') \in \mathcal{F} \quad \text{for any } A' \in \mathcal{F'}.  $$

The definition mirrors that of a continuous mapping between topological spaces. 

# Checking Measurability 

The definition is easy to understand, but hard to use. Sigma algebras are often found using generators, and we do not know exactly what their elements look like. If $\mathcal{F'}$ is given by $\sigma(\mathcal{G})$, we can not write out each $A' \in \mathcal{F'}$ to plug into the definition directly. We want to see whether a generator $\mathcal{G}$ can provide sufficient information on measurability. 

> **Proposition 1: (*check measurable*)**
> Let $f: (X, \mathcal{F})\to (X', \sigma(\mathcal{G}))$. Then $f$ is measurable if and only if:
> $$ f^{-1}(G) \in \mathcal{F} \quad \text{ for any } G \in \mathcal{G}.  $$

**Proof**
($\implies$) Measurability of $f$ implies $f^{-1}(A') \in \mathcal{F} \text{ for any } A' \in \sigma(\mathcal{G})$. Since $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \sigma(\mathcal{G})$, the statement is true applying to $G \in \mathcal{G} \subseteq \sigma(\mathcal{G}).$
($\impliedby$) Let $\Sigma = \{ A' \in \sigma(\mathcal{G}): f^{-1}(A') \in \mathcal{F} \}.$ 
Claim: $\Sigma$ is a sigma algebra. 
($\Sigma_{1}$) Since $\sigma(\mathcal{G})$ is a sigma algebra, $X' \in \sigma(\mathcal{G})$. Moreover, $f^{-1}(X')=X \in \mathcal{F}$ because $\mathcal{F}$ is a sigma algebra. Thus, $X' \in \Sigma$. 
($\Sigma_{2}$) If $A \in \Sigma$, then $f^{-1}(A) \in \mathcal{F}.$ $f^{-1}(A^c)= {f^{-1}(A)}^c \in \mathcal{F}$ because $\mathcal{F}$ is a sigma algebra. Thus, $A^c \in \Sigma$. 
($\Sigma_{3}$) Let $(A_{i}')_{i \in \mathbb{N}}$ be a sequence of sets in $\Sigma.$ For each $i$, $f^{-1}(A_{i}') \in \mathcal{F}$. Then $f^{-1}\left( \bigcup_{i\in \mathbb{N}}^{}A_{i}' \right) = \bigcup_{i}^{}f^{-1}(A_{i}') \in \mathcal{F}$ because $\mathcal{F}$ is a sigma algebra. Thus, $\bigcup_{i \in \mathbb{N}}^{}A_{i}' \in \Sigma.$

By assumption, $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \Sigma$. By the minimality of $\sigma(\mathcal{G})$, $\sigma(\mathcal{G}) \subseteq \Sigma.$ By the way we defined $\Sigma$, we checked any $A' \in\sigma(\mathcal{G})$ satisfies the requirement. 

$\square$

An immediate consequence follows from $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n) = \sigma(\mathcal{O}^n)$, where $\mathcal{O}^n$ is the family of open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$. 

> **Corollary: (*continuous functions*)**
> Let $f: \mathbb{R}^m\to \mathbb{R}^n$ be a continuous function. Then $f$ is measurable. 

**Proof**
For any open set $O^n$ in $\mathbb{R}^n,$ $f^{-1}(O^n)$ is open in $\mathbb{R}^m$ by continuity. Since $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^m) = \sigma(\mathcal{O}^m),$  $f^{-1}(O^n) \in \sigma(\mathcal{O}^m)=\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^m).$ Use the previous proposition. 

$\square$

We also consider compositions:

 > **Proposition 2 : (*composition*)**
 > Let $f: (X_{1}, \mathcal{F}_{1}) \to (X_{2}, \mathcal{F}_{2})$ and $g:(X_{2}, \mathcal{F}_{2}) \to (X_{3}, \mathcal{F}_{3})$ be measurable functions. $g \circ f: (X_{1}, \mathcal{F}_{1}) \to (X_{3}, \mathcal{F}_{3})$ is also measurable. 

**Proof**

For any $A_{3} \in \mathcal{F}_{3},$ 
$$
(g \circ f)^{-1} (A_{3}) = f^{-1}\left(g^{-1}(A_{3})\right) \in \mathcal{F}_{1}, 
$$
because $g^{-1}(A_{3}) \in \mathcal{F}_{2}$ by measurability of $g$. Apply measurability of $f$ to this set again. 

$\square$
# Push-forward Measure 

Suppose $(X, \mathcal{F})$ is equipped with a measure $\mu$. We can "push" this measure onto $(X', \mathcal{F}')$. 

> **Proposition 3: (*push-forward measure*)**
> Let $f:(X, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\to(X', \mathcal{F}')$ be a measurable mapping. $\mu\left(f^{-1}(F')\right)$ for each $F' \in \mathcal{F'}$ defines a measure on $(X', \mathcal{F}').$

**Proof**
($M_{0}$) $\mathcal{F'}$ is a sigma algebra.  
$(M_{1})$ $\mu\left(f^{-1}(\emptyset)\right)=\mu(\emptyset)=0$ because $\mu$ is a measure on $(X, \mathcal{F}).$
($M_{2}$) If $(A_{i}')_{i \in \mathbb{N}}$ is a sequence of disjoint sets in $\mathcal{F}'$:$$\mu\left(f^{-1}(\biguplus_{i \in \mathbb{N}}^{}A_{i}')\right)= \mu(\biguplus_{i \in \mathbb{N}}^{}f^{-1}(A_{i}'))=\sum_{i\in \mathbb{N}}^{}\mu(f^{-1}(A_{i}')).$$
The first equality follows because inverse images preserve unions and disjointness. $\mu$ being a measure on $(X, \mathcal{F})$ gives the second equality. 

$\square$

# Sigma Algebra Generated by a Mapping 

Consider $f: X \to \mathbb{R}$. On $\mathbb{R}$, a sigma algebra is already given by $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}).$ How can we find a proper sigma algebra to make $f$ measurable? An obvious candidate is the power set $\mathcal{P}(X)$.  $\mathcal{P}(X)$ is already a sigma algebra. For any $F' \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$, $f^{-1}(F')$ is a subset of $X$, thus $f^{-1}(F') \in \mathcal{P}(X)$. 

This is useless in many cases. Later, we will show that distributions can be constructed on $X=[0,1]$ equipped with Lebesgue measure. Although $\mathcal{P}(X)$ makes $f$ measurable, we are left with $([0,1], \mathcal{P}(X), Leb)$, which is not valid due to the inclusion of non-measurable sets. 

More generally, let $f:X\to X'$, where $(X',\mathcal{F}')$ is a measurable space. We want $f$ to be measurable. Let $f^{-1}(\mathcal{F}'):= \{ f^{-1}(F'): F' \in \mathcal{F}' \}.$  $f^{-1}(\mathcal{F'})$ is a sigma algebra on $X,$ and $f$ is measurable $w.r.t$ it. 

**Proof**
We show $f^{-1}(\mathcal{F'})$ is a sigma algebra. 
$(\Sigma_{1})$ Since $X' \in \mathcal{F'},$ $f^{-1}(X')= X \in f^{-1}(\mathcal{F'}).$
($\Sigma_{2}$) If $A \in f^{-1}(\mathcal{F'}),$ there exists some $A' \in \mathcal{F'}$ such that $A = f^{-1}(A')$. $A^c = {f^{-1}(A')}^c= f^{-1}(A'^c) \in f^{-1}(\mathcal{F'})$ because $A'^c \in \mathcal{F'}$. 
($\Sigma_{3}$) If $(A_{i})_{i \in \mathbb{N}}$ is a sequence of sets in $f^{-1}(\mathcal{F'}),$ then for each $A_{i}$, there exists some $A_{i}'\in \mathcal{F}'$ such that $A_{i} = f^{-1}(A_{i}').$  
$$
\bigcup_{i \in \mathbb{N}}^{} A_{i} = \bigcup_{i \in \mathbb{N}}^{} f^{-1}(A_{i}') = f^{-1}\left( \bigcup_{i \in \mathbb{N}}^{} A_{i}' \right) \in f^{-1}(\mathcal{F'} ).
$$
The last part holds since $\bigcup_{i \in \mathbb{N}}^{}A_{i}'$ is in $\mathcal{F'}.$

By the way we defined $f^{-1}(\mathcal{F'})$, $f$ is measurable $w.r.t$ it. 

Moreover, this is the smallest sigma algebra to make $f$ measurable. Suppose $f$ is measurable $w.r.t$ $\mathcal{F}_{}$. For any $F \in f^{-1}(\mathcal{F}'),$ $F = f^{-1}(F')$ for some $F' \in \mathcal{F'}$. Since $f$ is measurable $w.r.t$ $\mathcal{F}$, $F \in \mathcal{F}$. Thus, $f^{-1}(\mathcal{F}') \subseteq \mathcal{F}.$

$\square$

$\underline{Remark}:$ Note that we do not need $f$ to be measurable to make $f^{-1}(\mathcal{F}')$ a sigma algebra. This depends on whether $\mathcal{F'}$ is a sigma algebra. 

$\underline{Remark}:$ Denote $f^{-1}(\mathcal{F}')$ by $\sigma(f)$.

If we want to make multiple mappings on the same measure space measurable simultaneously, we consider
$$
\sigma(f_{i}, i \in I) = \sigma\left( \bigcup_{i \in I}^{} \sigma(f_{i})  \right),
$$
where $f_{i}: (X, \mathcal{F}) \to (X_{i}, \mathcal{F}_{i})$. 

$\underline{Remark}:$ Since a union of sigma algebras may not be a sigma algebra, we need to take the "sigma hull" to include all required sigma algebras, and ensure the final product is a sigma algebra. 
$\underline{Remark}:$ The domains of the $f_{i}$ must all be the same, but their codomains may differ.
